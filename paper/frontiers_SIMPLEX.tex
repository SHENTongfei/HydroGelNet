\begin{abstract}
\section{}
Hydrogel design relies on trial-and-error experimentation, where every candidate formulation must be synthesised and mechanically characterised; a model that predicts adhesion strength from monomer composition could accelerate screening of candidate formulations for biomedical and engineering applications. Existing deep-learning studies of hydrogels either use curve-derived targets or random splits that overestimate generalisation; none evaluates composition-to-property models under the realistic protocol in which a model trained on low-performance formulations must extrapolate to model-discovered high-performance formulations. Here we introduce SIMPLEX, a dual-modality residual network with interaction self-attention that maps six monomer molar fractions (plus their 15 pairwise interaction terms) to underwater adhesion strength, trained under Mixup, stochastic weight averaging and a range-domain constraint. We curate 180 training formulations (adhesion mean 47\,kPa) and evaluate on 161 formulations discovered by the original study's sequential model-based optimisation loop (adhesion mean 154\,kPa; 45\% beyond the training maximum) --- a model-guided migration to a high-performance composition region. Across 5-fold grouped cross-validation repeated over 5 seeds, SIMPLEX reached $R^2 = 0.709 \pm 0.077$, statistically tied with the strongest baseline (random forest, 0.719). Under extrapolation all models fail on absolute $R^2$ (target-range shift makes it undefined); on ranking, SIMPLEX significantly outperforms tree ensembles (external Spearman $\rho = 0.50$ vs 0.21 for random forest; paired-bootstrap difference $+0.18$, 95\% CI $[0.07, 0.31]$), ties the best linear model, and achieves the best top-20 screening precision (0.25 vs 0.10). Unlike the over-extrapolating linear baselines (which yield negative predictions), SIMPLEX predictions remain physically plausible (non-negative, in-range) while retaining ranking signal. Ablation identifies Mixup as the key regulariser, and permutation importance highlights the cation monomer and pairwise interaction terms. SIMPLEX supports material screening: candidate formulations are ranked so that only the top candidates need experimental synthesis, reducing iteration cost in data-scarce soft-materials research and development.

\section{Introduction}

Hydrogel design currently relies on trial-and-error experimentation, where each formulation must be synthesised and mechanically characterised \cite{liao2025nature,peppas2000hydrogels,calvert2009hydrogels}. A data-driven model that predicts adhesion strength from monomer composition could dramatically accelerate screening of candidate formulations for biomedical and engineering applications \cite{himanen2020datadriven,butler2018machine,cui2021wetadhesion}. The practical value of such predictions is direct: they narrow the experimental search space, prioritise which samples deserve costly follow-up, and expose which measured quantities actually carry information.

Three families of methods dominate current practice. Classical regularised linear models and kernel methods are stable at small sample sizes but cannot express the interactions between modalities that domain experts know to exist \cite{schmidt2019recent,ramprasad2017machine,aitchison1986}. Tree ensembles --- random forests and gradient boosting --- are the de-facto standard on tabular data, but they treat every column as an exchangeable scalar and provide no mechanism for sharing statistical strength across related endpoints \cite{breiman2001rf,friedman2001gbr}. Deep tabular networks promise both, yet in this regime they usually underperform: with a few hundred samples they overfit \cite{zhang2018mixup,izmailov2018swa}.

The failure modes are specific rather than generic. First, evaluation protocols that split rows at random allow measurements from the same source to appear in both training and test partitions, which inflates reported accuracy \cite{ovadia2019uncertainty,quinonero2009dataset}. Second, external validation on a cohort collected independently is rarely attempted, so it remains unknown whether learned relationships transfer at all \cite{mouret2017realitygap,shen2021ood,gulrajani2021domain,meredig2018can}. Third, and most damaging for soft-materials machine learning, no prior work evaluates composition-to-property models under the realistic out-of-distribution protocol in which a model trained on low-performance formulations must extrapolate to high-performance formulations discovered by an active-learning loop.

Here we present \textbf{SIMPLEX} (Simplex composition encoding with Interaction-aware attention, Multi-modal fusion, Pretraining-ready regularisation, Learnable domain constraints and EXtrapolation evaluation). SIMPLEX encodes the composition through two explicit modalities --- the raw monomer fractions and their pairwise interactions --- so that the model can represent composition synergy rather than relying on the network to invent it; refines the fused representation with residual blocks separated by an interaction self-attention layer; and predicts adhesion strength under Mixup, stochastic weight averaging and a range-domain constraint. Every design choice is an ablation switch, and any switch that does not pay for itself is removed from the final configuration.

Our contributions are: (i) a fully public, registration-free benchmark of 180 training and 161 model-discovered extrapolation formulations with 21 features across two modalities; (ii) SIMPLEX, a dual-modality residual architecture with interaction attention and small-data regularisation; (iii) an evaluation under repeated grouped cross-validation against equally tuned baselines, with a single, frozen-model pass on the extrapolation cohort; and (iv) evidence that SIMPLEX ranks candidate formulations significantly better than tree ensembles under extrapolation (external Spearman $\rho = 0.50$ vs 0.21) and achieves the best top-k screening precision, supporting material screening. \cite{varaprasad2017hydrogel,suneetha2019injectable} \cite{egozcue2003clr,cole2020design,wang2023hydrogelML,pawlowsky2015modeling} \cite{krueger2021outofdistribution} \cite{lin2023ohe,wang2020oodsurvey,hendrycks2019benchmark}

\subsection{Overall workflow and design rationale}



Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the workflow. Raw records are downloaded from a public repository, harmonised into a single feature space, and stored as an immutable array together with the group identifier of every sample. Model selection, hyper-parameter search and ablation are performed exclusively inside the internal cohort; the external cohort is untouched until the final configuration is frozen. \cite{jain2013materials,audus2019polymer,ward2017magpie,ward2018matminer}.

\subsection{Data acquisition}

\textbf{Internal cohort.} The internal cohort comprises 180 hydrogel formulations described by 21 features grouped into two modalities: \emph{monomer fractions} (six functional monomers on the composition simplex: nucleophilic HEA, hydrophobic BA, acidic CBEA, cationic ATAC, aromatic PEA and amide AAm) and \emph{pairwise synergy} (all 15 products $x_i x_j$, explicitly encoding monomer-pair interactions). The target is underwater glass adhesion strength $G$ (kPa), with training-region mean 46.9 and maximum 146.6~kPa. Samples originate from 180 distinct synthesis batches; all preprocessing (scaling) was fitted inside each cross-validation training fold only.

\textbf{External extrapolation cohort.} The external cohort comprises 161 formulations added in later iterations of the original study's sequential model-based optimisation (SMBO) loop \cite{liao2025nature}. These formulations occupy a model-discovered high-performance composition region: adhesion mean 154.2~kPa, maximum 353.3~kPa, with 45\% of external samples exceeding the training maximum. We therefore describe the protocol as a \emph{model-guided migration to a high-performance composition region} (target-value extrapolation) rather than an independent-laboratory cohort. The selection bias and range restriction inherent to the SMBO protocol are acknowledged; range-restricted Spearman $\rho$ is a conservative estimate of ranking ability \cite{tibshirani1996lasso}.

\subsection{SIMPLEX architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:modelarch} details the architecture. The six monomer fractions are encoded through two modalities: modality 1 uses the raw fractions; modality 2 adds the 15 explicit pairwise interaction terms. A linear embedding ($d = 64$) maps both modalities into the shared representation, which is refined by two residual blocks (dropout 0.2, LayerNorm) separated by an interaction self-attention layer (4 heads). A pooling-plus-linear head outputs the predicted adhesion strength (kPa, non-negative). Small-data regularisation comprises Mixup input interpolation ($\alpha = 0.4$), stochastic weight averaging, a range-domain constraint penalising out-of-range predictions, and early stopping on an inner validation split. The Transformer base was rejected because it diverged on 180 samples (internal $R^2 \approx -6$); self-supervised pre-training (masked-feature modelling, contrastive) was detrimental ($R^2 \approx -2.7$) and removed by ablation.

\subsection{Training and evaluation protocol}

The 180 internal formulations were evaluated with stratified 5-fold grouped cross-validation repeated over 5 seeds (25 models in the ensemble), with fold-internal preprocessing and an inner split reserved for early stopping. We benchmarked against eight classical baselines (ridge, elastic net, $k$-NN, support-vector regression with RBF kernel, histogram gradient boosting, random forest, multi-layer perceptron and the mean predictor), all tuned with an identical budget. Statistical inference used the Nadeau--Bengio corrected resampled $t$-test with Holm adjustment, cluster bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation tests. The 161 external formulations were evaluated exactly once after the architecture and all hyper-parameters had been frozen; because the external target range systematically exceeds the training range, absolute $R^2$ is undefined for every model (even a perfect ranker attains $R^2<0$ under target-value shift), so external generalisation is evaluated primarily on ranking metrics (Spearman $\rho$, top-k screening precision) and prediction-range behaviour.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Internal cross-validated performance}

Across 5-fold grouped cross-validation repeated over 5 seeds, SIMPLEX reached internal $R^2 = 0.709 \pm 0.077$ for adhesion strength, statistically tied with the strongest baseline (random forest, $R^2 = 0.719$; corrected $p = 1.0$, Cohen's $d = -0.14$). SIMPLEX outperformed the remaining baselines (support-vector regression 0.695, histogram gradient boosting 0.692, $k$-NN 0.687, ridge 0.541, elastic net 0.535, MLP 0.370). Figure~\ref{fig:cv} shows per-fold performance, error distributions and learning curves; Table~\ref{tab:internal} summarises all metrics.

\subsection{Ablation}

Ablation over 20 components (2 seeds $\times$ 3 folds) identified multimodal fusion ($\Delta R^2 = +0.093$), Mixup ($+0.067$), the residual blocks ($+0.062$) and interaction attention ($+0.019$) as the components carrying the signal; the domain constraint and SWA were neutral ($+0.000$ and $+0.008$). Sixteen candidate mechanisms (contrastive pre-training, masked-feature modelling, SAM, EMA, $R$-Drop, feature noise, MC-dropout, gated fusion and others) did not pay for themselves and were removed from the final model.

\subsection{Extrapolation to model-discovered formulations}

On the 161 model-discovered external formulations, every model attains $R^2 < 0$ (SIMPLEX $-0.93$; random forest $-1.53$), confirming that absolute prediction fails under target-value extrapolation. On ranking, SIMPLEX achieves the best external Spearman $\rho = 0.501$ (95\% bootstrap CI $[0.369, 0.619]$), significantly outperforming tree ensembles (random forest $\rho = 0.211$, CI $[0.164, 0.464]$; paired bootstrap difference $+0.183$, CI $[0.066, 0.311]$, $P(\Delta>0) = 0.999$) and statistically tying the best linear model (elastic net $\rho = 0.494$; difference CI $[-0.157, 0.097]$). SIMPLEX achieves the best top-20 screening precision (0.25 vs 0.10 for random forest and 0.05 for elastic net) and top-30 precision (0.37 vs 0.07 and 0.17). Prediction ranges reveal a mechanistic distinction: random forest compresses predictions into $[26, 109]$~kPa (tree models are locked near the training maximum), elastic net over-extrapolates to $[-82, 232]$~kPa (non-physical negative predictions), whereas SIMPLEX keeps predictions in $[16, 109]$~kPa --- physically plausible, non-negative and in-range --- while retaining ranking signal. Figure~\ref{fig:ext} reports these results; Figure~\ref{fig:rank} shows the external ranking with bootstrap intervals.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Cross-validated permutation importance (Figure~\ref{fig:interp}) ranks the cationic monomer ATAC first (importance 0.222), followed by interaction terms (ATAC$\times$PEA 0.099, BA$\times$CBEA 0.072, BA$\times$PEA 0.050) and the hydrophobic monomer BA (0.055). The dominance of the cation monomer and pairwise terms is consistent with the electrostatic/hydrophobic synergy known to drive underwater adhesion, and with the SMBO-discovered high-performance region enriched in the hydrophobic--aromatic (BA--PEA) combination.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What does the deep model contribute?}



The results show a structural rather than a numerical advantage. Internally, SIMPLEX matches the best tree ensemble (0.709 vs 0.719, statistically tied); externally, it significantly outperforms tree ensembles on ranking (Spearman 0.50 vs 0.21) and achieves the best top-k screening precision. The mechanism is visible in the prediction ranges: tree models extrapolate by nearest-neighbour interpolation and are locked near the training maximum, collapsing ranking signal; linear models over-extrapolate into non-physical negative territory; SIMPLEX --- trained with Mixup over the composition simplex, a continuous mapping and a range-domain constraint --- keeps predictions physically plausible while preserving ordering. For material screening, the model's value lies in ranking candidate formulations so that only the top candidates need experimental synthesis, and in doing so at a 2--5$\times$ better top-k precision than tree ensembles under target-value extrapolation.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations warrant emphasis. (i) The external cohort originates from the same laboratory (later SMBO iterations), so it is a model-guided extrapolation benchmark rather than an independent cohort; selection bias and range restriction are inherent. (ii) Absolute values of adhesion strength in the extrapolation region systematically exceed the training range, so ranking metrics are the primary external metrics; $R^2$ is dominated by mean shift in this regime. (iii) Microstructural variables (cross-link density, network topology) are not recorded in the public dataset and are implicitly averaged over. (iv) The internal difference from random forest is not statistically significant, and the linear baselines attain lower external RMSE through strong shrinkage --- both facts are reported transparently.

\subsection{Future work}

Promising directions include extending evaluation to independent-laboratory cohorts, injecting microstructure descriptors as additional modalities, closing the active-learning loop by using the model to propose the next formulations to synthesise, and multi-target extension (a preliminary single-task validation on modulus reaches parity with random forest, $R^2 = 0.396$ vs 0.392).

\section{Conclusion}

SIMPLEX is a dual-modality residual architecture with interaction attention and small-data regularisation that maps monomer composition to underwater adhesion strength, evaluated under a model-guided extrapolation protocol in which the model must rank formulations beyond the training value range. Under this protocol SIMPLEX significantly outperforms tree ensembles on ranking (external Spearman 0.50 vs 0.21), achieves the best top-k screening precision, and keeps predictions physically plausible unlike over-extrapolating linear baselines, while matching the strongest baseline in-distribution. The framework supports material screening in data-scarce soft-materials research and development.
